# Drosophila sleep, spontaneous brain activity, and biological data for validating a simulated fly visual system

State as of 18 September 2026. All findings below are tied to a URL; anything I could not verify with a fetched source is listed under Gaps rather than stated as fact.

## Key Question 1: Is there experimental evidence of internally generated visual-like activity in fly optic lobes during sleep or quiescence?

### Takeaway
Fly sleep is now firmly multi-staged ("active sleep" with wake-like brain activity in the first ~5 min; "quiet"/deep sleep with proboscis extensions, reduced activity and elevated arousal thresholds), and whole-brain recordings show the optic lobes carry sleep-specific LFP signatures. However, I found no study demonstrating replay/reactivation of visual-scene-like patterns inside the optic lobes during sleep; the only insect "replay" evidence is reactivation of central-brain dopaminergic/mushroom-body memory circuits. Sleep-related gating of vision has so far been localized to the central brain (EPG/central complex, predictive-processing responses), not the optic lobes.

### Cited Findings

**Sleep stages and brain-activity signatures**
- van Alphen, Semenza, Yap, van Swinderen, Allada (2021, Science Advances 7:eabc2999) identified a discrete deep sleep stage, "proboscis extension sleep" (PES), defined by repeated stereotyped proboscis extensions/retractions, with highly elevated arousal thresholds and decreased brain activity; blocking proboscis extensions increased injury-related mortality and reduced waste clearance; sleep deprivation reduced clearance and rebound sleep increased sleep, PE events and clearance. The authors propose waste clearance as a core, ancient function of deep sleep. — [Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abc2999); press summary [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2021/01/210120151044.htm)
- Tainton-Heap, Kirszenblat, Notaras, Grabowska, Jeans, Feng, Shaw, van Swinderen (2021, Current Biology) recorded brain activity during spontaneous sleep and dFB (dorsal fan-shaped body)-induced sleep: optogenetic dFB activation promotes sustained wake-like levels of neural activity while flies become unresponsive to touch; this induced sleep corrected visual-attention deficits caused by sleep deprivation. The authors call this a "paradoxical" sleep in which brain activity resembles wake but sensory responsiveness is profoundly suppressed. — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:33238155&resultType=core&format=json); [Cell Press page](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(20)31658-4); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33238155/)
- In the same line of work, flies can be asleep after the first minute of spontaneous inactivity; during the first ~5 minutes of sleep the brain shows wake-like activity ("active sleep"), followed by "quiet sleep" after 5–10 min. — [Current Biology dispatch "Sleep: Imaging the Fly Brain Reveals New Paradoxes" (2021)](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(20)31869-8)
- Anthoney et al. (2023, eLife 12:e88198) "Experimentally induced active and quiet sleep engage non-overlapping transcriptional programs in Drosophila": active sleep (brain as active as wake, but quiescent and low arousal) and quiet sleep (reduced brain activity, rhythmic proboscis extensions) induced optogenetically engage distinct gene-expression programs, arguing they are separate physiological states rather than one continuum. — [eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/88198); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37910019/)
- A 2026 review, "Current Evidence for Sleep States in Drosophila: Findings and Implications" (Current Sleep Medicine Reports; PMC13031250; PubMed 41907494), surveys these stage definitions. I could not fetch its full text (CAPTCHA/paywall), so its specific claims are not reported here. — [PMC listing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13031250/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40675-025-00352-w)
- Homeostatic regulation of deep sleep and molecular correlates of sleep pressure (eLife 2024/2025, article 91355) extends the deep-sleep (PES) work. — [eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/91355)
- Calcium imaging: neuronal activity and reactivity decline during sleep and rise during wake in many fly neurons (Bushey et al., PNAS 2015). GCaMP6f activity is elevated in the first 5 min of quiescence then reduced, with decreased connectivity among active neurons during sleep vs wake (search summary citing Tainton-Heap 2021 / Jagannathan). — [PNAS 2015](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1419603112)
- Whole-brain calcium imaging and whole-brain electrophysiology protocols during sleep and wake were published in 2024 (Cold Spring Harbor Protocols, Jagannathan/van Swinderen lab). — [PubMed 38148168](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38148168/); [PubMed 38148172](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38148172/)

**R5 ring neurons, slow waves and sensory filtering**
- Raccuglia et al. (2019, Current Biology) found compound delta (slow-wave) oscillations in the sleep-regulating R5 ellipsoid-body network; their power increases with sleep need and varies diurnally; single R5 units are synchronized via NMDA-receptor coincidence detection driven by cholinergic and glutamatergic input; TuBu neurons projecting to R5 can drive slow synchronous oscillations. — [Current Biology 2019](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(19)31120-0); dispatch [Slow Waves Quiet the Fly's Mind](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(19)31170-4)
- Raccuglia, Suárez-Grimalt, ..., Owald (2025, Nature, "Network synchrony creates neural filters promoting quiescence in Drosophila"): coherent slow-wave activity at 0.5–1.5 Hz between R5 (sleep-need) and helicon cells (locomotion-promoting visual neurons), plus dFSB and downstream EPG head-direction neurons. R5 oscillations reduce responsiveness to visual stimuli by rhythmically cancelling helicon drive onto EPG: daytime EPG shows robust visual activation; at night visually evoked depolarization is attenuated; optogenetic R5 activation at 1 Hz in daytime reproduced the night-like attenuation. Nocturnal coherence is abolished in period mutants; sleep deprivation increases R5–helicon correlation in the morning. In prolonged darkness 95.8% of recordings showed synchronized profiles vs 58.5% under LD; visual input facilitates a "shifted" (open-filter) state where helicon leads R5 by 50–200 ms. Methods: dual-colour voltage imaging (ArcLight in R5, Varnam in dFSB/helicon) at 80–100 fps, in vivo and ex vivo. — [PMC12527942](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12527942/); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09376-2); bioRxiv precursor [2022.03.11.483976](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.03.11.483976v2.full)
- Subtype-specific roles of ellipsoid-body ring neurons in sleep regulation (J Neurosci 2023). — [J Neurosci](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/43/5/764)
- Four SpsP neurons act as an integrating sleep regulation hub (Science Advances 2025). — [Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads0652)

**Optic lobes during sleep**
- Van De Poll & van Swinderen (2025, J Exp Biol 228:jeb250165): 16-channel LFP arrays spanning optic lobes to central brain, recorded over days in tethered flies with 10 Hz blue/green flicker and rare colour deviants. Responses to the high-probability carrier were unchanged in sleep, but sleep significantly decreased responses to deviant stimuli in central and intermediate channels; optic-lobe channels showed no sleep-related change in deviant processing. The effect was strongest during deep sleep with rhythmic proboscis extensions. The whole brain "including the outer optic lobes" shows distinct sleep LFP features, yet optic lobes keep processing deviants regardless of state, so sleep gating of surprise sits in the central brain. — [PMC12188248](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12188248/); [JEB](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/228/11/jeb250165/368170/); bioRxiv [2025.01.09.632265](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.01.09.632265v1.full)
- Sleep deprivation impairs visual responsiveness/attention in flies, and dFB-induced sleep restores it (Tainton-Heap 2021; see above). — [Cell Press](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(20)31658-4)
- Phototransduction itself promotes day sleep (2025/2026, PMC12972697): photoreceptor activation signals via histamine/acetylcholine to clock neurons directly or via optic-lobe networks; optic-lobe glia may regulate synaptic plasticity and visual processing through octopamine signalling, influencing sleep. — [PMC12972697](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12972697/)

**Replay / reactivation in insects**
- Dag et al. (2019, eLife): consolidation of long-term courtship memory is mediated by reactivation during post-learning sleep of the same dopaminergic neurons involved in acquisition; specific fan-shaped-body neurons induce sleep after learning and activate those dopaminergic neurons. This is central-brain (MB/dopamine) reactivation, not optic-lobe replay. — [eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/42786); [PMC6428568](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6428568/)

### Inferences
- The best-supported "internally generated" activity in fly sleep is (a) wake-like global activity during the first minutes of sleep (active sleep) and (b) coherent 0.5–1.5 Hz slow waves in central-complex networks (R5/helicon/dFSB/EPG). Neither has been shown to contain scene-like or motion-like structure in the optic lobes.
- Because optic-lobe deviant responses are unchanged during sleep (Van De Poll 2025), the visual periphery appears to remain a largely feed-forward, stimulus-driven system during sleep; "dreaming" in a simulation would have to be justified as central-brain (central complex / mushroom body) reactivation propagating via visual centrifugal neurons (VCNs; catalogued in Nern 2025) rather than as evidence-based optic-lobe replay.
- A defensible modelling choice is: sleep = (i) removed/attenuated photoreceptor input, (ii) 1 Hz R5-type slow-wave modulation gating central targets, (iii) octopamine low. Any optic-lobe "replay" must be flagged as hypothesis, not biology.

### Gaps
- No study found (2019–Sept 2026) that images T4/T5, Mi1, Tm or lamina neurons during natural sleep, or reports reactivation of visual-evoked patterns in the optic lobe during sleep; this appears genuinely unexplored, not merely unindexed.
- Whether fly sleep has a REM-homologous stage remains a matter of terminology ("paradoxical"/active sleep); I could not access the 2026 review's verdict on REM-like classification.
- Yap et al. 2017 (Nat Commun) reported oscillatory (7–10 Hz) brain activity in spontaneous and induced sleep; I did not fetch it this session, so its details are unverified here.
- Jagannathan et al. 2024 multichannel-LFP sleep-staging paper is referenced by secondary summaries ("optic lobes display distinct sleep features") but I did not retrieve the primary article.

## Key Question 2: What drives spontaneous/ongoing activity in the fly brain and optic lobe, and which open datasets could fit/validate a connectome-constrained visual model?

### Takeaway
Whole-brain imaging (light-field, SCAPE, volumetric 2-photon) consistently shows that the dominant "spontaneous" signal is locomotion: walking/flailing raises activity across ~40% or more of the brain, with dopaminergic and octopaminergic (incl. an optic-lobe–PENP component) neurons among the most strongly recruited; residual non-behavioural activity is high-dimensional and spatially clustered. Several of these datasets are public (DANDI 000727; CRCNS K01J97ZN), and the optic-lobe connectome plus model code (flyvis, neuPrint optic-lobe:v1.1) are open, but curated open two-photon datasets of T4/T5, Mi1/Tm3 or L1–L5 responses to natural scenes are scarce; the Pang et al. 2024 Dryad/Zenodo L1/L2 dataset is the clearest example.

### Cited Findings

**Spontaneous / ongoing activity and what drives it**
- Aimon, Katsuki, Jia, Grosenick, Broxton, Deisseroth, Sejnowski, Greenspan (2019, PLOS Biology): light-field microscopy of near-whole-brain calcium and voltage activity in behaving flies; brain activity increased globally when the fly walked but not when it groomed; the global walking increase was particularly strong in dopamine neurons; PCA/ICA maps matched subneuropil anatomy and sometimes specific cell types; spontaneous voltage-activity patterns were observed. — [PMC6395010](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6395010/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30768592/)
- Aimon, Cheng, Gjorgjieva, Grunwald Kadow (2023, eLife 12:e85202): during spontaneous and forced walking, cholinergic, glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons are activated in most regions; dopaminergic neurons are "strongly and broadly activated" (MB and central-complex components); octopaminergic activation is widespread, with an optic-lobe–periesophageal-neuropil (OL-PENP) component strongly correlated with walking; serotonergic neurons are less globally activated and inhibited in the anterior VLP during walking. Forced vs spontaneous walk brain-state patterns are highly similar (cosine similarity 0.985–0.993). Most components activate after walk onset, i.e. activity is coupled to movement rather than a persistent baseline. Code: https://github.com/sophie63/Aimon2022; raw imaging data: http://dx.doi.org/10.6080/K01J97ZN (CRCNS). — [eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/85202)
- Schaffer et al. (2023, Nature Communications; "Flygenvectors"): SCAPE microscopy of all cells in a large brain volume during spontaneous behaviour; most neurons correlate or anticorrelate with running and flailing on timescales from seconds to a minute; grooming elicits a weaker global response; residual activity not explained by behaviour is high-dimensional and reflects small, spatially organised neuron clusters that may map onto genetically defined types. — [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-41261-2); bioRxiv [2021.09.25.461804](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.09.25.461804v1)
- Brezovec, Berger, Hao, Chen, Druckmann, Clandinin (2024, Current Biology): pan-neuronal GCaMP6f volumetric 2-photon at 1.8 Hz (~1.6 M voxels/volume) during walking; locomotor signals in ~40% of the brain; distinct topographic maps for forward vs angular velocity in navigation, memory, sensory and motor regions; temporal trajectories sweeping across maps, including signals anticipating future movement; maps registered to a connectome. — [PubMed 38242122](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38242122/); [Cell Press](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(23)01763-3)
- High-speed whole-brain imaging in Drosophila (Nature Communications 2026) is a newer method paper in this line. — [Nature Communications 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-72437-1)
- Intrinsic brain activity gates visual information during behavioural choices (older LFP work, van Swinderen lab). — [PMC3012687](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3012687/)

**Open datasets**
- DANDI:000727 "Mapping the Neural Dynamics of Locomotion across the Drosophila Brain" (Brezovec, Berger, Druckmann, Clandinin): 609.6 GB, 18 NWB files, 9 subjects, two-photon volumetric imaging during spontaneous walking, CC-BY-4.0, version 0.240106.0043 (Jan 2024). — [DANDI 000727](https://dandiarchive.org/dandiset/000727)
- CRCNS dataset for Aimon et al. 2023 whole-brain light-field imaging: http://dx.doi.org/10.6080/K01J97ZN — [eLife data statement](https://elifesciences.org/articles/85202)
- Pang et al. (2024, Current Biology, "A recurrent neural circuit in Drosophila temporally sharpens visual inputs"): L1/L2 voltage imaging (ASAP2f) including naturalistic stimuli derived from fly walking; all data on Dryad https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.ngf1vhj4c, code on Zenodo https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.13367946. — [PMC11769683](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11769683/)
- flyvis (TuragaLab): official PyTorch implementation of Lappalainen et al. 2024 with pretrained model ensembles downloadable via CLI, Sintel-derived naturalistic stimuli, and tutorials for flash responses and moving-edge responses. — [GitHub](https://github.com/TuragaLab/flyvis); [docs](https://turagalab.github.io/flyvis/)
- Lappalainen et al. (2024, Nature; PMC11525180): connectome-constrained network for 64 cell types of the motion pathway, unknown parameters optimised by deep learning; predictions agreed with experimental measurements across 26 studies. The model spans ~45,000 neurons across 721 columns; synaptic signs inferred from transcriptomics. — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1038/s41586-024-07939-3&resultType=core&format=json); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39261740/); [Janelia news](https://www.janelia.org/news/researchers-combine-the-power-of-ai-and-the-connectome-to-predict-brain-cell-activity)
- Nern et al. (2025, Nature; PMC12119369): FIB-SEM connectome of the right optic lobe of a male fly, ~53,000 neurons in 732 types (about half new), with neurotransmitter identity; released split-GAL4 lines matched to the catalogue and a computational framework. Data in neuPrint "optic-lobe:v1.1" (~50,000 neurons, 8 nm isotropic). Cell Type Explorer: https://reiserlab.github.io/male-drosophila-visual-system-connectome/ ; code: https://github.com/reiserlab/male-drosophila-visual-system-connectome-code. — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1038/s41586-025-08746-0&resultType=core&format=json); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08746-0)
- Companion Nature 2024 papers: "Neuronal parts list and wiring diagram for a visual system" (FlyWire optic lobe) and "Predicting visual function by interpreting a neuronal wiring diagram". — [Nature 2024 parts list](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07981-1); [Nature 2024 predicting function](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07953-5)
- Whole-brain "effectome" (Nature 2024) offers connectome-derived linear dynamics as an additional validation target. — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07982-0)
- Max Planck (Borst lab) 2-photon imaging of T4/T5 is described on their methods page, but no public dataset link was found. — [MPI Biological Intelligence](https://www.bi.mpg.de/borst/2P-imaging)
- Drews et al. (2020, Current Biology, "Dynamic Signal Compression for Robust Motion Vision in Flies"): contrast tuning curves and rapid contrast adaptation measured in L1, Mi1, Tm3 etc. — a validation target (values in paper, dataset link not located). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982219313752)

### Inferences
- For fitting/validating a model, the practical stack is: connectome (neuPrint optic-lobe:v1.1 / FlyWire) + flyvis pretrained ensembles as a reference model + published tuning curves (26 studies summarised by Lappalainen 2024) + the one open raw optic-lobe recording set (Pang 2024 L1/L2, Dryad). Whole-brain datasets (DANDI 000727, CRCNS K01J97ZN) are useful for state/locomotion modulation of the optic lobes but have coarse (1.8 Hz, voxel-level) resolution.
- "Spontaneous" activity in the fly is mostly behaviour-locked; a simulated "idle" fly should therefore show low, sparse activity unless locomotion or neuromodulator state is switched on.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the exact list of free parameters in Lappalainen 2024 (per-cell-type membrane time constants, resting potentials, synaptic-strength scaling) from the paper itself (Nature/bioRxiv blocked, 429/403); the abstract only says "unknown parameters" were optimised. Confirm from PMC11525180 full text.
- No DANDI/Zenodo/Figshare dataset of two-photon T4/T5, Mi1 or Tm responses to natural scenes was found; recordings underlying classic papers (Maisak 2013, Arenz 2017, Strother 2017, Gruntman 2018) appear to be available only on request.
- No functional (physiological) recordings were released with Nern 2025 as far as the abstract indicates; the release is anatomical plus genetic lines.

## Key Question 3: Which neuromodulatory/state variables most change visual processing and could serve as "dream" knobs, and what biophysics does the connectome not specify?

### Takeaway
The best-characterised state knob is octopamine during flight/walking: it raises gain in motion-vision neurons, shifts T4/T5 temporal tuning to higher frequencies by speeding their inputs (Mi1, Tm3, Mi4, Mi9), and is necessary and sufficient for the flight-induced visual boost. Beyond neuromodulation, a connectome omits photoreceptor light-adaptation dynamics, lamina gap junctions and recurrent feedback kinetics (L1/L2 biphasic sharpening), rapid contrast adaptation, and circadian/sleep-pressure slow-wave gating — each must be parameterised separately.

### Cited Findings

**Octopamine / behavioural state**
- Suver, Mamiya, Dickinson (2012, Current Biology): pharmacological octopamine evokes responses in quiescent flies that mimic flight; octopamine neurons are both necessary and sufficient for the flight-induced boost of visual (motion) responses. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982212012559)
- Octopaminergic modulation of the visual flight-speed regulator (JEB 2014): sensitivity to large-field optic flow is increased by octopamine release during flight, enhancing active regulation of forward speed. — [JEB](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/217/10/1737/14000/)
- Strother et al. (2018, PNAS "Behavioral state modulates the ON visual motion pathway"): behavioural state alters baseline activity and temporal tuning of T4 and its inputs Mi1, Tm3, Mi4, Mi9; central octopaminergic neurons input to Mi4 and increase its excitability; octopamine neurons are required for sustained responses to fast- but not slow-moving stimuli in walking flies. — [PMC5776785](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5776785)
- Arenz et al. (2017, "The temporal tuning of the Drosophila motion detectors is determined by the dynamics of their input elements"): octopamine-receptor activation (chlordimeform) shifts T4/T5 temporal tuning to higher frequencies, explained by concomitant speeding of input elements. — [ResearchGate listing of Arenz 2017](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315951341_The_Emergence_of_Directional_Selectivity_in_the_Visual_Motion_Pathway_of_Drosophila); summary in [Neuromodulation of insect motion vision review (JCP A 2019)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00359-019-01383-9)
- Gain increase by locomotion or chlordimeform is much stronger at high than low temporal frequencies (blowfly/fly LPTC work; adaptation-level dependent). — [PMC4443275](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4443275/)
- Flexible filtering by neural inputs supports motion computation across states and stimuli (Current Biology 2021). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982221013178)
- 2026 Current Biology cellular/functional dissection of the octopaminergic/tyraminergic system: synaptic connections exist between AL2i1 octopaminergic neurons and T4/T5; OA modulates gaze stabilisation and object tracking by increasing response gain. — [Current Biology 2026](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(26)01007-9); [PMC13523668](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13523668/)
- Olfactory input also modulates motion-vision circuitry (Wasserman et al. 2015). — [PMC4331282](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4331282/)
- Walking recruits dopaminergic neurons broadly and an optic-lobe-associated octopaminergic component (Aimon 2019, 2023; see Q2). — [eLife 2023](https://elifesciences.org/articles/85202)

**Sleep-pressure / circadian gating**
- R5–helicon 0.5–1.5 Hz coherent slow waves attenuate visually evoked EPG responses at night or under sleep pressure; visual input shifts the network to an "open filter" state (Raccuglia 2025; see Q1). — [PMC12527942](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12527942/)
- Sleep leaves optic-lobe deviant responses intact but suppresses central-brain deviant responses, strongest in deep (PES) sleep (Van De Poll 2025; see Q1). — [PMC12188248](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12188248/)

**Biophysics a connectome does not specify**
- Photoreceptors R1–6 depolarise to light increments and have monophasic impulse responses; with increasing mean intensity both the transduction cascade and membrane speed up, accelerating voltage responses (Juusola & Hardie 2001, light adaptation at 25 °C; temperature increases reliable-signalling bandwidth). — [PubMed 11134228](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11134228/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/12195182_Light_adaptation_in_Drosophila_photoreceptors_I_Response_dynamics_and_signaling_efficiency_at_25_degrees_C)
- Network adaptation in the eye (photoreceptor–LMC feedback) whitens frequency spectra and flattens voltage distributions over seconds, requiring both light-gated and synaptic feedback conductances (Zheng et al. 2009, PLOS One I & II). — [PMC2628724](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2628724/); [PMC2628722](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2628722/)
- Lamina: within a cartridge the six R1–R6 axons are extensively coupled by symmetrical gap junctions (mainly in the distal third of the cartridge); R1–6 synapse onto L1–L3; electrical synapses have been reported between L1 and L2. — [Cell Tissue Res (gap junctions coupling photoreceptor axons)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00236726); [PMC2481516 (medulla input terminals)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2481516/); [PMC11769683](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11769683/)
- Pang et al. 2024: L1 and L2 receive near-identical photoreceptor input but L1 is recurrently connected with L5, Mi1, Tm3, C2 and L2 with Dm6, Dm15, Dm17, Dm19 (indirectly Dm1, C2, C3); chemical synapses are essential for the biphasic (temporally sharpening) response (tetanus toxin abolishes it); L2 fit constants τv = 0.0131 s, τy = 0.561 s; feedback reduced motion-blur temporal offset for natural images; the authors state the connectome "does not specify" the functional role of these pathways. — [PMC11769683](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11769683/)
- Parallel acetylcholine signals facilitate LMC repolarisation and modulate visual behaviour (J Neurosci 2021) — another non-connectomic (receptor-type) determinant. — [J Neurosci](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/41/10/2164)
- Contrast adaptation is widespread in the early visual system (L1 and downstream Mi1/Tm3 measured), rapidly adjusting sensitivity to local contrast and improving downstream velocity estimation (Drews 2020); heterogeneous temporal-contrast adaptation exists in direction-selective circuits (Matulis 2020). — [ScienceDirect Drews 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982219313752); [PMC7003801](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7003801)
- Insect gap junctions remain a "knowledge gap" for connectomics (Current Biology 2022 dispatch); electrical synapses are not resolved by standard EM connectomes. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982222005024)
- Inhibitory columnar feedback neurons (C2/C3-type) contribute to motion processing (eLife 2025). — [eLife 108529](https://elifesciences.org/articles/108529)
- Single-neuron biophysics can produce multi-output computation in the visual system (bioRxiv 2025), i.e. point-neuron models under-specify some cells. — [bioRxiv 2025.02.02.636137](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.02.02.636137v1.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- Candidate "state knobs" for a simulation, ranked by evidence: (1) octopamine level (flight/walk): multiplicative gain on Mi1/Tm3/Mi4/Mi9 → T4/T5 plus a shift of input time constants toward faster kinetics; (2) locomotion signal (drives dopaminergic and OL-PENP octopaminergic activity brain-wide); (3) sleep pressure/circadian phase: a 0.5–1.5 Hz slow-wave gate acting on central targets of visual projection neurons (EPG, helicon), not on the lamina/medulla; (4) mean luminance: photoreceptor speed and lamina adaptation; (5) local contrast: fast gain control in L1→Mi1/Tm3.
- Elements a plan must add beyond the connectome: photoreceptor temporal filter with luminance-dependent speed; R1–6 and L1–L2 gap junctions; recurrent lamina/medulla feedback kinetics (τ ≈ 13 ms fast, ≈ 0.56 s slow for L2); contrast adaptation; neurotransmitter-receptor kinetics (ACh, GABA, glutamate, histamine); neuromodulator gain fields (octopamine, dopamine); sleep/circadian slow-wave gating.

### Gaps
